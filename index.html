Who We Are

A Human-Centered AI Harms Investigation Firm

AI Forensic Agents was founded on a conviction that has grown urgent: when artificial intelligence systems cause harm — to individuals, communities, or democratic institutions — someone must investigate with rigor, independence, and an unwavering commitment to human dignity.

We are investigators, not evangelists. Our work begins where AI failures end: in the wreckage of automated decisions that denied healthcare, amplified discrimination, enabled surveillance, or eroded the foundations of informed consent.

We study history because history teaches us what happens when transformative technologies outpace accountability. The steam age offers a devastating and instructive parallel.

  • Forensic Rigor We trace AI harms to their root causes — data, design, deployment, and institutional failure.
  • Human-Centered Every investigation begins and ends with the people affected, not the systems that harmed them.

The Historical Parallel

The Age of Preventable Catastrophe

For most of the nineteenth century, Americans boarded steamboats knowing they might not survive the journey. Boiler explosions were not rare events — they were an epidemic. When one or more flues inside a boiler collapsed, the resulting rupture could destroy an entire vessel in seconds. Passengers died from the explosion itself, from scalding released steam, from drowning in the aftermath, and from fires that consumed the wreckage.

A Chronicle of Disaster

FEBRUARY 24, 1830
Steamboat Helen McGregor
Boiler explosion on the Mississippi River, near Memphis, Tennessee. One of the earliest recorded mass-casualty steamboat disasters in American history.
⬥ 33–60 killed
APRIL 25, 1838
Steamboat Moselle
Boiler explosion and fire near Cincinnati, Ohio. The Moselle was racing to depart ahead of a rival steamer when her overtaxed boilers detonated. Bodies were hurled hundreds of feet. The disaster prompted some of the first public calls for steamboat regulation.
⬥ 81–200 killed
OCTOBER 23, 1844
Steamer Lucy Walker
Boiler explosion on the Ohio River near New Albany, Indiana. The vessel was destroyed almost instantaneously.
⬥ 56–80 killed
DECEMBER 29, 1847
Steamer A.N. Johnston
Boiler explosion on the Ohio River near Maysville, Kentucky. Another mid-river catastrophe on one of the nation's busiest waterways.
⬥ 60–80 killed
DECEMBER 13, 1850
Steamer-Tug Anglo-Norman
Boiler explosion near New Orleans, Louisiana. The blast claimed scores of lives in the heart of America's busiest port.
⬥ 20–100 killed
APRIL 3, 1852
Steamboat Glencoe
Boiler explosion and fire at St. Louis, Missouri. The fire that followed the initial explosion compounded the death toll.
⬥ 40–62 killed
APRIL 9, 1852
Steamboat Saluda
Boiler explosion on the Missouri River at Lexington, Missouri — just six days after the Glencoe disaster. Many aboard were Mormon emigrants heading west.
⬥ 54–100 killed
JANUARY 27, 1855
Steamboat Pearl
Boiler explosion on the Sacramento River, Sacramento, California. The gold rush era brought hurried steamboat construction with little oversight.
⬥ 67–80 killed
FEBRUARY 27, 1859
Steamer Princess
Boiler explosion and fire on the Mississippi River near Baton Rouge, Louisiana. The fire that erupted after the blast made rescue nearly impossible.
⬥ ~70 killed
OCTOBER 12, 1865
Steamer Yosemite
Boiler explosion on the Sacramento River at Rio Vista, California. Even after the Civil War — which should have impressed the horrors of mass death upon the national conscience — the explosions continued.
⬥ 55–68 killed
FEBRUARY 2, 1866
Steamboat W.R. Carter
Boiler explosion on the Mississippi River near Island No. 98. The single deadliest disaster in this record, yet it scarcely changed the regulatory landscape.
⬥ 123–215 killed
AUGUST 27, 1871
Steamboat Ocean Wave
Boiler explosion at Point Clear near Mobile, Alabama. Decades of disasters, and still no comprehensive federal safety framework.
⬥ 34–75 killed
SEPTEMBER 9, 1932
Steamboat Observation
Boiler explosion on the East River near Riker's Island, New York. Over a century after the Helen McGregor, boilers were still killing people — this time in the shadow of Manhattan's skyscrapers.
⬥ 72 killed
The Argument

Then and Now

The structural dynamics that allowed steam boiler explosions to persist for over a century are remarkably similar to the dynamics enabling unchecked AI harms today.

Steam Age (1830–1932)

  • Profit incentives drove operators to over-pressure boilers and skip inspections
  • No federal regulatory framework for decades despite mounting deaths
  • Victims were often passengers with no knowledge of the risks they faced
  • Industry resisted safety standards as costly and unnecessary
  • Expertise was siloed — few could inspect what they could not see
  • Accountability was diffuse: builders, operators, and owners all pointed elsewhere

AI Age (Present Day)

  • Market pressure drives deployment of AI systems before adequate testing
  • Regulatory frameworks lag far behind the pace of AI deployment
  • Those harmed by AI decisions often have no knowledge of algorithmic involvement
  • Industry frames regulation as innovation-killing overreach
  • Algorithmic opacity means few can audit what they cannot understand
  • Accountability is diffuse: developers, deployers, and vendors all point elsewhere
The steam boiler epidemic ended not because industry self-corrected, but because society demanded enforceable standards. AI harms will follow the same trajectory — the only question is how many will be harmed before we act.
